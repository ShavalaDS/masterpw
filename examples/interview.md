# Interview — Marta Bianchi, daily regional commuter

Interviewed at Milano Centrale just after her morning arrival, twenty-five minutes, recorded with consent. Verbatim transcript: `interview-transcript.md`. Quotes below reference the transcript timestamps.

## Who she is

Thirty-four, product manager for a fintech in Milan. Lives in Monza, works five days a week near Porta Nuova — a fifteen-minute walk from Milano Centrale. Has done this commute for four years, always the same pattern: regional train just before 08:00, arrive, coffee, walk to the office.

## The 07:52 is never on time

Her train is the 07:52 from Monza. In her words, it is "never on time" — most days one to five minutes late, "often enough that I stopped trusting it":

> "I check the app before I even leave home. If it says delayed, I change when I shower. That's how much it runs my morning." — [00:04:12]

She checks two apps — the operator's app and a second one for live train positions — because "the official one updates late." If the two disagree, she trusts the one showing the train's position on the map.

## The moment of the delay

When a delay hits while she's already at the station, the sequence is always the same: app first, board second, then a decision about whether to message her team. The hard part is not knowing how long it will last:

> "The board says 'delayed'. Delayed how much? Five minutes I wait, thirty minutes I need to tell someone. And I find out which one it is only when it's already too late to be useful." — [00:11:38]

What she wants is not more data — the delay is already visible in three places. She wants it explained: what happened, how long, and whether an alternative makes sense *for her*, in one place:

> "I don't need another app. I need the app I have to tell me: this is what happened, this is the new arrival, and here's what you can do about it." — [00:17:05]

## Waiting time, and what she does with it

The wait at Monza is short, the wait at Centrale is the walk. The real dead time is on the train when it is held between stations — no information at all, just "we are stopped, we don't know why". During waits she listens to podcasts, but chooses the length based on how risky the morning feels:

> "If the train's already acting weird, I don't start a forty-minute episode. I scroll. I don't enjoy the scroll, it's just what fits the uncertainty." — [00:14:47]

## Signals — reading, not evidence

Mapping to the brief's pain points:

- **Uncertainty about arrival times** — the strongest signal in this interview: not the delay itself, but the *unquantified* delay ("delayed how much?").
- **Information scattered across apps** — she cross-checks two apps against each other and the board; the truth is assembled, not delivered.
- **Recovering from a delay** — the moment of the delay is where the anxiety concentrates: deciding whether to warn her team without knowing the magnitude.
- **Boredom during waiting** — present but secondary: dead time shapes her media choices, not her stress.

Strongest candidate moment from this interview: **the delay explanation** — one plain-language answer to "what happened, how long, what can I do about it."
