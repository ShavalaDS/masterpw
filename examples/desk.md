# Desk research — How long Europeans spend commuting

The macro context for Brief 1: how much time Europeans spend on the daily commute, how that varies by country, and how commuters feel about it. Collected during the Lesson 02 hands-on with a web-connected agent; every figure below traces to a source, and one source is still pending verification — marked as such.

## The European picture: 48 minutes a day

According to a 2026 survey by talent management company SD Worx, reported by Euronews, the **daily average commute across Europe sits at 48 minutes** [S1]. The average masks a wide spread between countries — roughly half an hour at the short end, over an hour at the extreme.

## The country spread

- **Italy and Slovenia** keep daily travel times relatively brief, **around 35 minutes** [S1] — Italy sits well below the European average.
- **Sweden (57 minutes)** and **Ireland (56 minutes)** report hefty averages [S1].
- **Belgium crosses the one-hour mark at 61 minutes a day** [S1], driven by the highest rate of "super-commuters" in Europe: **13% of its workforce spends two hours or more on the road, and 14% travel over 120 km daily** [S1].

## Time is not distance

The same average can hide opposite realities. The **Netherlands and Romania both average 53 minutes**, but for opposite reasons: Dutch workers cover **63 km on average**, leaning on efficient long-distance infrastructure, while Romanian workers spend the same time on **much shorter distances**, where congestion eats the clock [S1]. For a commuting feature, this distinction matters: the pain is not always in the kilometres — it can be entirely in the friction.

## The longer structural view

The official Eurostat picture (EU Labour Force Survey, 2019 — pre-pandemic, the latest ad-hoc collection of this kind) [S2]:

- **61.3%** of EU-27 workers commuted one-way in **less than 30 minutes**; **26.3%** between 30 and 59 minutes; **8.1%** for 60 minutes or more.
- The **EU average one-way commute was 25 minutes**; **Italy among the shortest at 21 minutes**.
- **City dwellers commute longer** (average 27 minutes) than those living in towns, suburbs or rural areas (24 minutes) — density moves the pain to the cities.
- The higher the educational attainment, the longer the commute: 40.4% of high-education workers commute 30 minutes or more, against 27.7% of low-education workers.

## How commuters feel about it

The counter-intuitive finding from the SD Worx data: even in Belgium — where the average commute (61 minutes) surpasses the ~57 minutes people generally consider "acceptable" — **only 34% of workers view that time as wasted, and demand for remote work among Belgian employees sits at just 32%**, below the international average [S1]. The commute is tolerated, even defended. The problem for design is not the time itself, but the *quality* of that time — which is exactly where uncertainty, scattered information and dead waiting land.

## Signals — reading, not evidence

For Brief 1 (Milan rail commuters):

- **Italy's average is short (~35 minutes daily, 21 minutes one-way)** — the Italian commuter's problem is unlikely to be the length of the ride; it is the friction inside a short ride: delays, uncertainty, crowding. This supports focusing on a *moment*, not the whole journey.
- **The tolerance paradox** — commuters accept long commutes but resent unpredictability — points at the brief's "reduce uncertainty / make transit feel predictable" line, and at the strongest signals from the interviews: the unquantified delay ("delayed how much?").
- **Eurostat's data is from 2019** — pre-pandemic. Treat the structural figures as background, not as a description of today's commuter. Marked medium confidence for this reason.
